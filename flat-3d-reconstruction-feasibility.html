Technical feasibility report

Reconstructing flats in 3D from listing photos

What is possible today when the only inputs are an unlabelled bag of estate-agent photographs and, sometimes, a floor plan — no poses, no calibration, no captions, no capture control.

01The verdict, briefly

The literal version of your idea — take any listing, press a button, receive a complete, metrically correct, freely walkable, photorealistic 3D flat — is not achievable at reliable quality today. Not because any single piece is impossible, but because one specific piece has no signal to work with, and several others are much more fragile on real estate-agent photographs than their benchmark numbers suggest.

A slightly different product, however, is achievable now, and I think it is the more interesting one anyway. The move is to stop treating this as a reconstruction problem and start treating it as an inference problem with a reconstruction component: recover what the photographs genuinely support, infer the rest from strong structural priors, and choose a presentation format that is honest about which is which. Concretely, a scaled architectural shell derived from the floor plan and per-room layout estimation, with photorealistic Gaussian splats anchored inside each room, navigated by teleporting between waypoints rather than free roaming. That is buildable in roughly four to six months by a small team, and it lands very close to what buyers actually want from a virtual visit.

The strategic observation from the market scan is worth stating early: every incumbent in this space (Matterport, Zillow, Giraffe360, Realsee, Cupix) controls the capture. They sell a camera, or a capture app, or a photographer. Nobody has a production-grade product that works from photographs that already exist. That gap is real, and it is real for a reason — it is genuinely hard. But it is a gap, and the pieces needed to close it have moved a long way in the last eighteen months.

02Decomposing the problem

"Photos in, 3D flat out" is nine distinct problems wearing one coat. Separating them is the most useful thing you can do early, because their maturity levels differ enormously, and a plan that treats them as one monolith will fail at whichever one is weakest without telling you which.

Sub-problemStatusWhat that means
1. Triage
Which image is a floor plan, a room, an exterior, a stock photo?		 ✓ Solved A vision-language model or a fine-tuned classifier handles this at high accuracy. Cheap, boring, reliable.
2. Calibration

3. Grouping

4. Per-room geometry

5. Room layout
Walls, floor, ceiling as a clean polygon		 ~ Workable Mature for 360° panoramas, considerably weaker for ordinary perspective shots, and benchmark scores are measured on empty rooms. Furniture is the enemy.
6. Floor plan parsing
Raster plan → vector rooms, doors, dimensions		 ~ Workable Good research results on clean plans; European agency plans are stylised and varied. OCR of the printed room areas is the underrated part.

8. Metric scale

9. Appearance
Photorealistic surfaces		 ! Fragile Gaussian splatting from 5–15 unposed indoor photos produces recognisable rooms with visible artefacts. The unphotographed 60% has to come from somewhere, and that somewhere is a product and legal decision, not a technical one.

Notice the shape of it: nothing is impossible, one thing is genuinely unsolved, and the rest are "works well on benchmarks, degrades on real inputs". That second category is where projects like this quietly die — not at a wall, but in a long slow accumulation of 80%-success stages multiplying down to a 20%-success pipeline. Guarding against that is mostly an evaluation discipline problem, which is why §9 puts the eval harness before any of the interesting work.

03Where the difficulty actually lives

The first wall: rooms have no spatial relationship in the photographs

Within a room, two photos taken from opposite corners share walls, a ceiling plane, a floor plane, a window. That is enough for a modern pointmap model to place them relative to each other, even with almost no pixel overlap. Between rooms, there is nothing. A photo of the kitchen and a photo of the bedroom share no observed surface at all. No amount of model capability recovers a quantity for which there is no observation.

There are exactly three ways through this, and it is worth being clear that they are the only three:

  1. The floor plan. If a plan is present, it is the global coordinate system. Every room polygon is given, adjacency is given, and the problem collapses from "estimate 6-DoF poses for N rooms" to "assign N reconstructed rooms to M plan polygons" — a small, well-conditioned matching problem. This is why I would treat the plan as the spine of the whole product rather than an optional extra.
  2. Doorway chaining. Occasionally you see the same doorway from both sides: from the hall looking into the bedroom, and from the bedroom looking back. That shared aperture gives you a relative pose between two rooms. In practice this is available for perhaps a third of adjacencies in a well-photographed listing, and almost never enough to close a full graph.
  3. Generation. Synthesise a plausible layout consistent with the room count, room types and total surface area. This produces something that looks like a flat and is not the flat. It has a legitimate use — as an explicitly-labelled schematic — and an illegitimate one, which is presenting it as a measurement.

The design consequence

Because option 1 is so much stronger than the others, the architecture in §5 is built around a floor-plan-first spine with graceful degradation, rather than a photo-first pipeline that treats the plan as a bonus. A listing with a plan gets a real flat. A listing without one gets a set of accurately reconstructed rooms with an inferred, visibly-marked arrangement. Those are two different products and should look different to the user.

The second wall: estate agent photography is adversarial to reconstruction

This is the finding I would most encourage you to internalise, because it is invisible from the research literature and obvious the moment you look at real listings. Property photographers are trained to produce images that are exactly wrong for multi-view geometry:

PracticeWhy it existsWhy it breaks the pipeline
Ultra-wide lenses (14–24 mm equivalent)Makes rooms look largerExtreme perspective; feature descriptors degrade at the frame edges; heavy distortion that has usually been partially corrected in a non-invertible way

None of these is individually fatal. Together they mean that a pipeline validated on ScanNet or Structured3D will disappoint on real listings by a margin large enough to be mistaken for a bug. Budget for an image-conditioning stage and for a rejection path — some photos should simply be excluded from geometry and used only for texture.

The third wall: benchmark numbers do not survive contact

Room layout methods report 80%+ 3D IoU. They report it on PanoContext, Stanford 2D-3D and Structured3D — datasets of empty or synthetically-furnished rooms, shot as clean panoramas. ZInD, the most realistic large dataset, is explicitly composed of unfurnished homes ✓ VERIFIED. A furnished flat photographed at 16 mm with a sofa occluding the wall-floor junction across half the frame is a different problem. The research pass surfaced an estimate of a 20–40% accuracy drop when these methods are applied directly to messy real-world photos ○ REPORTED; I would treat that as directionally right and measure it yourself early rather than trusting it.

04State of the art, stage by stage

What follows is organised as the pipeline runs, because that is how you will have to build and debug it. For each stage: what the best current methods are, what they actually deliver, and where they break.

4.1  Triage — sorting the bag of images

The input is 10–30 unlabelled JPEGs containing rooms, exteriors, street scenes, detail shots, possibly a floor plan, possibly stock photos of the neighbourhood, possibly the same room three times. Everything downstream depends on getting this right, and fortunately it is the easiest stage.

Two viable approaches. A zero-shot vision-language model classifying each image into {floor_plan, interior, exterior, detail, marketing, other} plus a room type, which needs no training data and can be prompted to also flag "this looks virtually staged" or "this contains a mirror". Or a fine-tuned classifier over CLIP/SigLIP embeddings, which is cheaper at volume. Places365 and MIT Indoor67 are the classical reference datasets; a small real-estate-specific classifier called RE-Tagger exists in the literature ○ REPORTED but you will do better fine-tuning a modern backbone on a few thousand of your partner agency's images.

Expect 75–90% top-1 on room type in the general case, with hallway-versus-bedroom and dining-versus-living being the honest confusions. The VLM route has a useful property here: it can return "I'm not sure" and a confidence, and an uncertain image can be routed to texture-only use rather than poisoning the geometry.

4.2  Calibration and image conditioning

EXIF is usually gone. The research pass found that major portals and their image CDNs strip metadata on upload ○ REPORTED, which matches what you would expect for privacy reasons — listing photos often carry GPS. Plan for no focal length, no camera model, nothing. Blind calibration is not an optimisation, it is the only path.

GeoCalib (ECCV 2024, cvg/GeoCalib) is the strongest single-image calibration method: it combines a learned prior with a geometric optimisation over vanishing points and horizon, and supports both pinhole and fisheye parameterisations without retraining ○ REPORTED. Indoor Manhattan scenes are its favourable case, since walls give it clean orthogonal structure. The classical vanishing-point approach (NeurVPS and descendants) remains a sensible cross-check.

In practice you may not need a separate calibration stage at all, because the pointmap models in §4.4 estimate intrinsics themselves as part of their forward pass. The argument for running GeoCalib anyway is as a sanity gate: if GeoCalib and the pointmap model disagree wildly about field of view, that photo has probably been perspective-corrected and should be treated with suspicion.

The conditioning steps I would build regardless: exposure and white-balance alignment across the photos of a single room (they were often shot minutes apart with different settings, and both SfM and splatting assume otherwise), and a rejection classifier for composites.

4.3  Grouping photos into rooms

Two stages, retrieval then verification. For retrieval, global descriptors — DINOv2 features, or a purpose-built place-recognition head such as AnyLoc or MegaLoc — produce candidate same-room pairs. For verification, a local matcher establishes whether there is real geometric correspondence.

The matcher landscape as of mid-2026: LightGlue for speed, RoMa / RoMa v2 for dense accuracy on hard pairs, and MASt3R for matching posed as a 3D problem rather than a 2D one, which is the right framing for wide baselines ○ REPORTED. OmniGlue reports AUC@5° of 5.8 on ScanNet-1500 against SuperGlue's 5.1 ○ REPORTED — note how low those absolute numbers are. Indoor wide-baseline matching is hard, and the literature is candid about it.

Practical expectation

On textured rooms with 60–120° viewpoint change, expect 40–70% of pairs to verify correctly. On minimalist interiors with white walls, considerably less. Symmetric rooms and repeated tiling produce confident wrong matches, which are worse than no match. Budget for a VLM adjudication step ("are these two photographs of the same room? What do you see in both?") — it costs a few cents per listing and catches failures that geometry cannot.

One relevant recent paper: Room Scene Discovery and Grouping in Unstructured Vacation Rental Image Collections (arXiv 2507.00263, July 2025) addresses exactly this task ○ REPORTED. Worth reading closely; it is the nearest thing to prior art on your specific grouping problem.

4.4  Per-room geometry — the part that recently became possible

This is where the field has moved most, and it is the reason your idea is worth attempting in 2026 and would not have been in 2023. The pointmap foundation models — DUSt3R and its lineage — replaced the "detect features, match, bundle-adjust" pipeline with a single forward pass that regresses 3D points and camera parameters directly. Crucially, they work with two or three images and minimal overlap, precisely the regime where COLMAP returns nothing at all.

The honest caveat on pointmap models

An independent evaluation on aerial photogrammetry found these models substantially less accurate than COLMAP when overlap is good — roughly 0.06 m error for COLMAP versus 1–2 m for the learned methods — and reported VGGT accumulating up to 42 m of camera drift over a 191-image sequence ○ REPORTED. Read that as: pointmap models win decisively in the sparse, wide-baseline, low-overlap regime where classical SfM fails outright, and lose where classical SfM succeeds. For your problem you are firmly in the first regime, so this is the right family — but it also means you should not expect survey-grade geometry, and you should not feed it fifty images and expect the errors to average out. Per-room batches of 3–8 images is the sweet spot.

None of these models has published results on real estate listing photographs. You will be the first to measure that.

Fisheye. All of these assume a pinhole model. There is early work on conditioning pointmap models on wide-FOV camera models ○ REPORTED, but the practical answer is to undistort first with GeoCalib and accept some loss at the frame edges.

Monocular fallback. When a room has exactly one photograph — common for bathrooms and hallways — you need single-image geometry. The strong options are MoGe-2 (Microsoft, metric point maps plus intrinsics from one image), Depth Pro (Apple, Apache-2.0, metric depth with an internally-estimated field of view, sub-second inference), UniDepth V2 and Metric3D v2. Note that Depth Anything V2 is relative depth by default and its larger checkpoints are CC-BY-NC-4.0 — only the Small model is Apache-2.0 ✓ VERIFIED. That licence split catches people out.

4.5  Room layout — turning a point cloud into architecture

A pointmap is not a room. You need the wall planes, the floor, the ceiling, and ideally a clean polygon. This matters more than it first appears: the layout polygon is what makes the model measurable, what lets you cull stray Gaussians later, and what you match against the floor plan.

The panorama-based literature is mature — HorizonNet, DuLa-Net, HoHoNet, LGT-Net, AtlantaNet — with reported 3D IoU in the 80s on cuboid rooms and around 72% on genuinely non-rectangular ones ○ REPORTED. AtlantaNet is the one to look at if your stock is Parisian haussmannien and other old, irregular buildings, since it drops the requirement that walls meet at right angles. Perspective-image layout estimation is markedly weaker, roughly 60–75% 3D IoU on clean cuboid benchmarks.

The more promising route for your inputs is plane-based: fit planes to the pointmap (RANSAC on normals is often enough), classify them as floor/ceiling/wall by orientation, and intersect them into a polygon. PlaneMVS, NOPE-SAC and PlaneFormer are the learned versions, and NOPE-SAC in particular solves camera pose and plane reconstruction jointly from sparse views ○ REPORTED. There is also Plane-DUSt3R (ICLR 2025), which adapts DUSt3R specifically to unposed room layout reconstruction ○ REPORTED — the closest published work to the shape of your stage 4–5.

For the output representation, look at Meta's SceneScript, which emits architecture as a sequence of tokens (make_wall, make_door, …) rather than a mesh. The idea is excellent — a compact, editable, human-readable room description that you can validate, snap to right angles, and extrude cleanly. The code is not released ○ REPORTED, but the representation is worth stealing.

4.6  The floor plan channel

Given the argument in §3, this stage carries more weight than its size suggests. Four things need to happen.

Detect it. Trivial — a VLM or classifier picks the plan out of the image set.

Vectorise it. CubiCasa5K is the reference dataset and the basis of most working systems. The research pass surfaced a cluster of 2024–2026 methods — RoomFormer, PolyRoom, Raster-to-Graph, and newer transformer and diffusion approaches reporting ~88% room F1 on CubiCasa5K and ~98% on synthetic Structured3D ○ REPORTED. The gap between those two numbers is the whole story: performance on real, stylised, agency-drawn plans is meaningfully worse than on clean synthetic ones, and European plans are more varied than the datasets. Expect to fine-tune.

Scale it. Here is the part that no research paper solves and that your market hands you for free. French listings print room areas directly on the plan — Séjour 24,5 m², Chambre 11,2 m² — and the total surface is a legal requirement of the listing itself. OCR those numbers and you have not one but several independent scale constraints, plus a cross-check. This is a far more reliable route to metres than any learned method, and it is sitting in plain sight.

Match photos to rooms on it. This is a genuine research problem, and there is a genuinely relevant recent paper: C3Po (arXiv 2511.18559), which predicts pixel-level correspondences between ground-level photographs and floor plans, trained on a new dataset of 90K photo/plan pairs across 597 scenes ✓ VERIFIED. Zillow's LASER and CVPR 2024's F³Loc attack the adjacent problem of localising a camera within a floor plan ○ REPORTED. For a first version you do not need any of them: matching by room type label, area, aspect ratio and window count, solved as a Hungarian assignment, will place most rooms correctly and can defer the ambiguous ones to a human.

4.7  Global assembly

Covered in §3 — with a plan this is assignment, without one it is inference. The one addition worth making is that assembly should be treated as a single global optimisation rather than a chain of local decisions. You have a rich set of soft constraints available at once: room polygons from §4.5, plan polygons from §4.6, room type labels, the total surface area, door and window positions on both the plan and in the photos, ceiling height consistency across rooms, and gravity alignment. Solving them jointly is both more accurate and more diagnosable than pipelining them, because the residuals tell you which listing is problematic and why.

4.8  Metric scale — the surface-area trick

Metric monocular depth models now genuinely output metres. UniDepth and MoGe-2 report δ₁ above 98% on NYUv2 ○ REPORTED, which sounds excellent until you translate it: δ₁ counts pixels within 25% of ground truth. At a typical 3–4 m indoor viewing distance that is a tolerance of nearly a metre. Reported practical error for these models indoors is on the order of 10–15%. A flat advertised at 68 m² coming out at 78 m² is not a product.

The fix, and I think it is the neatest idea in this report

Treat scale as a single scalar to be solved for, not something each stage estimates independently, and constrain it with everything you know at once:

  • The advertised surface area. In France the loi Carrez makes a measured surface figure a legal requirement of the sale listing. It is published, in metres squared, on every listing. It is a direct global constraint on total floor polygon area, and it is free.
  • Door heights. Standard interior door leaves are ~2.04 m across the EU. Detect doors, measure them in model units, and you have one anchor per doorway.
  • Ceiling heights. Residential norms cluster at 2.5–2.7 m in modern construction and considerably higher in older Parisian stock — a weaker prior, but useful as a plausibility check.
  • Other standard dimensions: kitchen counters at 0.90 m, light switches at ~1.10–1.30 m, stair risers, standard tile sizes.
  • The metric depth models themselves as a prior with a wide but honest uncertainty.

This is a tiny weighted least-squares problem — one unknown, half a dozen observations with known variances — and it is extremely robust. My estimate is that it brings total area error to roughly ±3–5% when the Carrez figure is available, against ±15% from depth models alone. It also gives you a free quality signal: when the constraints disagree badly, something upstream is wrong, and you can flag the listing rather than shipping a bad model.

4.9  Appearance — Gaussian splatting from very few views

For "photorealistic views", 3D Gaussian splatting is the right representation, and the sparse/unposed variants have matured. InstantSplat uses a pointmap model for initialisation and jointly optimises poses and Gaussians, reporting roughly 30× speedup over the SfM-then-splat pipeline. Splatt3R goes pose-free from stereo pairs. FreeSplat is specifically aimed at indoor free-view synthesis from 2–3 views. DepthSplat couples depth estimation and splatting in both directions. MVSplat reports 26.4 dB PSNR on RealEstate10K from two input views ○ REPORTED. Depth-regularised variants — DNGaussian, SparseGS — consistently buy 10–30% LPIPS improvement in the sparse regime ○ REPORTED.

The practical consensus from the research pass: 6–12 views per room is where results become acceptable, and below 3 views floaters and smearing dominate ○ REPORTED. A typical listing gives you 2–4 photos of the main room and 1–2 of everything else, so you will spend a lot of time in the difficult part of that curve.

A trick worth building in

Floaters — stray Gaussians floating in space — are the dominant sparse-view artefact, and they are what makes a splat look broken rather than merely soft. If you have already computed a room layout polygon in §4.5, you have a bounding volume, and you can simply cull every Gaussian outside it plus a small margin. Geometry and appearance reinforcing each other like this is the main argument for running both stages rather than picking one.

Meshes from splats, if you want them: SuGaR extracts editable meshes in minutes via Poisson reconstruction, and 2D Gaussian Splatting, Gaussian Opacity Fields and GS2Mesh are the surface-aligned alternatives ○ REPORTED. For your use case I would not bother — the architectural shell should come from the layout polygon, which is cleaner, lighter and actually measurable, with splats layered inside it for realism.

4.10  Completion — what to do about the unseen 60%

Every room has surfaces no camera saw, and they are usually the wall behind the photographer. Options, in increasing order of both usefulness and risk:

  1. Leave them empty. With waypoint navigation you can often simply never look that way. Honest, cheap, and surprisingly acceptable.
  2. Flat-shade them from the room's dominant wall colour, sampled from the observed surfaces. Reads as "no data" without looking broken.
  3. Texture infill — extend the observed wall texture across the unobserved plane. Geometrically truthful (the wall really is there, from the layout), only the surface appearance is invented.
  4. Generative view synthesis. Stability's Stable Virtual Camera takes 1–32 images and generates long trajectories, but is released under a non-commercial licence ○ REPORTED. Google's CAT3D/CAT4D are research. Tencent's HunyuanWorld is open-source and panorama-oriented ○ REPORTED. World Labs' Marble is the most product-like: it accepts text, images, video or coarse 3D layouts and exports Gaussian splats, collider and visual meshes, or video ✓ VERIFIED. Its own documentation is candid about the failure mode you would care about — "the back of a table or the far corner of a room is not as crisp as the room's centre" ✓ VERIFIED. Pricing is not public.

Where the line is

Options 1–3 invent appearance. Option 4 invents content — furniture, fixtures, a view through a window. For property marketing that is a different category of thing, and the regulatory direction of travel is against it (§10). My recommendation: build 1–3 for the product, keep 4 in the lab, and if you ever ship it, confine it strictly to surfaces whose geometry is known and mark it visibly in the viewer. The rule that keeps you safe is simple — never generate anything that changes the perceived size, layout or condition of the property.

4.11  Delivery in the browser

This stage is comfortably solved, which is a relief.

Renderer. Spark, built by World Labs, is a Gaussian splat renderer for Three.js that composites splats and ordinary meshes in one scene with correct depth, and reads PLY, SOGS, SPZ, SPLAT and KSPLAT ✓ VERIFIED. That hybrid capability is exactly what your architecture needs — mesh shell, splats inside. Alternatives: Three.js gained a native WebGPU splat renderer in r186 ○ REPORTED, PlayCanvas has strong splat and compression support, and @mkkellogg/GaussianSplats3D is widely used but its maintainer now points people at Spark ○ REPORTED.

Formats and budgets. Raw PLY for a 4M-Gaussian scene is around 1.4 GB; SPZ (Niantic, open-sourced) brings that to roughly 160 MB, and SOGS to 80–140 MB with streaming support ○ REPORTED. Khronos has standardised a glTF Gaussian splatting extension ○ REPORTED, which is a good sign for the ecosystem. For the mesh shell, glTF with Draco geometry compression and KTX2/Basis textures routinely achieves 95%+ size reduction.

Mobile is the real constraint. Reported figures: 1–3 M splats on iPhone, 1–2 M on Android, with iOS Safari memory ceilings around 100–200 MB and no catchable exception when you exceed them — the tab simply dies ○ REPORTED. Practical target: 200–500 K splats per room at 30 fps on a mid-range phone, and a total payload in the single-digit megabytes. That means one room loaded at a time, which fits waypoint navigation perfectly.

Why waypoint navigation is an architectural decision, not a UX one

Matterport, Zillow and Giraffe360 all use teleport-between-waypoints with a dollhouse overview and a floor plan minimap. It is easy to read that as convention. It is actually a load-bearing engineering choice: a waypoint viewer only needs the scene to look right from the waypoints and along the sightlines between them. Free roam demands complete, watertight geometry everywhere, including behind the sofa and inside the wardrobe. Given that you will never have data for those places, waypoint navigation is not a compromise — it is the format that matches what your pipeline can actually produce. Choose it deliberately and early, because it also tells you which viewpoints to optimise the splats for.

05A proposed architecture

Nine stages, each with a defined fallback, each producing an artefact you can inspect and score independently. The design principles behind it: the floor plan is the spine; geometry and appearance are computed separately and reinforce each other; scale is one global variable solved once against all available constraints; and every stage emits a confidence that survives to the viewer.

Stage 0 Ingest and triage

VLM or fine-tuned classifier over each image → type, room label, quality flags (mirror present, likely virtually staged, likely composite), plus a perceptual-hash dedup. Listing text is parsed for surface area, room count and floor.

Output: a manifest. Fallback: none needed — this stage is reliable.

↓
Stage 1 Conditioning

GeoCalib for intrinsics and distortion → undistort to pinhole. Exposure and white-balance alignment within each candidate room group. Images that fail plausibility checks are demoted to texture-only.

Fallback: if calibration is implausible, pass the raw image through and let the pointmap model estimate intrinsics, with a lower confidence weight.

↓
Stage 2 Grouping into rooms

DINOv2 or MegaLoc global descriptors → candidate pairs → LightGlue or RoMa verification → graph clustering. VLM adjudicates low-confidence pairs.

Fallback: a photo that groups with nothing becomes a singleton room and is routed to the monocular path in stage 3.

↓
Stage 3 Per-room geometry

MapAnything (Apache checkpoint) over each group of 3–8 images → poses, metric point map, confidence masks.

Fallback for singletons: MoGe-2 or Depth Pro monocular point map. Fallback for failures: drop to layout-only for that room.

↓
Stage 4 Structure extraction

Plane fitting on the point map → floor, ceiling, wall planes → intersection into a room polygon, regularised under an Atlanta-world assumption (right angles preferred, not required). Door and window apertures detected and placed on the wall planes.

Fallback: if plane fitting fails, fall back to a cuboid fitted to the point cloud extent, flagged as approximate.

↓
Stage 5 Floor plan channel (parallel to 2–4)

Vectorise the plan → room polygons, adjacency graph, door positions. OCR the printed room labels and areas. This runs independently and does not block the photo path.

Fallback: no plan → skip; stage 6 switches to the degraded branch.

↓
Stage 6 Global assembly

With a plan: Hungarian assignment of reconstructed rooms to plan polygons, scored on room type match, area ratio, aspect ratio, window count and position. Then a joint refinement over rotations and translations.
Without a plan: build what graph you can from shared doorways, then synthesise a layout constrained by room count, types, adjacency conventions and total surface area — and mark the entire result as an inferred arrangement in the viewer.

This is the stage most likely to produce a wrong-but-confident answer. Instrument it heavily.

↓
Stage 7 Global scale solve

One weighted least-squares problem over a single scale factor: advertised surface area (strongest weight where available), detected door heights against a 2.04 m prior, ceiling height priors, standard fixture dimensions, metric depth predictions. Residuals become the listing's quality score.

Fallback: no advertised area → lean on door heights, and widen the published uncertainty rather than hiding it.

↓
Stage 8 Appearance

Per room: Gaussian splatting (gsplat) initialised from stage 3 poses and points, depth-regularised, a few hundred iterations, then culled against the stage 4 polygon. The architectural shell mesh is built from the polygon and texture-projected from the photos where they cover it; unobserved surfaces get flat-shaded or texture-extended fill.

Fallback: rooms with one photo get shell-plus-projected-texture only, no splats.

↓
Stage 9 Packaging and delivery

Shell as glTF with Draco and KTX2 (hundreds of KB). Splats as SPZ or SOGS, budgeted per room. Three.js plus Spark for hybrid rendering. Viewer offers waypoint navigation, a dollhouse overview, a floor plan minimap, and a visual treatment that distinguishes photographed surfaces from inferred ones.

Progressive load: shell first, then the splats for the room you are standing in.

Two properties of this design are worth calling out. First, it degrades in steps rather than collapsing: a listing with no plan and one photo per room still produces a scaled schematic walkthrough, which is a legitimate product. Second, every stage's output is inspectable, so when a listing looks wrong you can see which stage produced the error rather than re-running the whole thing and hoping.

06The stack, and the licensing minefield

Practical advice on licensing

Keep a single file in the repository that records, for every model you load: the code licence, the weights licence, the training data licences the authors disclose, and the date you checked. This field re-licenses things frequently — VGGT gained a commercial checkpoint after release, DINOv2 moved to Apache after its initial release — so a check from six months ago may be either too pessimistic or dangerously stale. Re-verify before each release.

07What remains genuinely unsolved

Six things that no amount of engineering effort will make go away, listed so you can decide which ones you are willing to live with.

1. Inter-room placement without a floor plan

Not a modelling gap — an information gap. The photographs do not contain the answer. Any system that appears to solve this is inferring, and should say so. Mitigation: make the plan the spine; degrade visibly when it is absent; consider offering the user a thirty-second drag-and-drop room arrangement step, which turns an unsolvable problem into a trivial one.

2. Wide-baseline matching on textureless interiors

A white-walled minimalist bedroom photographed twice from opposite corners may share no matchable structure at all. Pointmap models help enormously because they reason about geometry rather than texture, but they are not magic. Mitigation: lean on plane correspondence rather than point correspondence, and accept a per-room failure rate.

3. Radiometric chaos

HDR compositing, flash-ambient blending and per-image grading break the photometric consistency both SfM and splatting assume. Mitigation: normalise aggressively before geometry, and accept that splat quality will be limited by inconsistent lighting between views of the same wall.

4. Mirrors and glass

A classical failure with no clean solution. Bathrooms and hallways — the rooms most likely to have a single photo and a large mirror — are the worst case. Mitigation: detect mirrors explicitly with segmentation, mask them out of geometry, and treat them as flat reflective planes in the final render.

5. The unobserved majority of every room

No pipeline recovers what was never photographed. The choice is between showing nothing, showing a plausible-looking invention, or choosing a navigation format where it does not arise. Mitigation: waypoint navigation plus honest visual treatment of inferred surfaces.

6. Benchmark-to-reality transfer

Every accuracy figure in §4 was measured on data unlike yours. Mitigation: this is the one you can actually fix, and §9 puts it first — build your own evaluation set from real listings with ground truth you have measured, before building anything else.

One more, on the VLM-as-reasoning-glue hypothesis

It is tempting to hope a frontier VLM can supply the missing spatial priors — "which room is this next to", "how high is this ceiling". Worth tempering: the VSI-Bench work (Thinking in Space, arXiv 2412.14171) found that multimodal models' spatial reasoning is competitive but distinctly sub-human, that it is the primary bottleneck on that benchmark, and — notably — that chain-of-thought, self-consistency and tree-of-thoughts all fail to improve spatial performance ✓ VERIFIED. What did help was having the model explicitly build a cognitive map during answering. Read that as: use the VLM for semantics and adjudication, where it is strong, and do not ask it for metric quantities. It is a good judge and a poor tape measure.

08What it costs to run

Compute turns out not to be the interesting cost. Working from a listing of fifteen photographs across five rooms, on a rented L40S or A100-class GPU at roughly $1–2 per hour:

Total8–15 GPU-minutes≈ €0.30–1.50 per listing

One of the research passes returned a much higher figure of €6–20 per property. That estimate assumed 20 minutes for pointmap inference on 10–20 images, which was true of DUSt3R-era pairwise optimisation but is not true of the current feed-forward models — these run in seconds. I would plan around the lower figure and measure it properly in phase 0.

The cost that actually matters

If 25% of listings need three minutes of human review — checking that rooms landed in the right places, nudging an assignment, rejecting a bad reconstruction — that is €0.30 per listing at €25/hour of reviewer time. In other words, human review costs about the same as all the compute combined, and it scales linearly with volume while compute costs fall. Every point of accuracy you gain in stages 6 and 7 converts directly into margin. That is a useful thing to know before deciding where to spend engineering effort: shaving GPU seconds is close to worthless, and reducing the review rate from 25% to 10% is worth more than any infrastructure optimisation you could do.

Delivery bandwidth is the third line. At 5 MB per listing and a few hundred views over its life, CDN costs land in the same order of magnitude as compute — meaningful but not structural.

09A staged plan, with kill criteria

The sequencing principle: build the thing that is both most valuable and most verifiable first. That is not the photorealism — it is the measurable shell. A scaled, navigable, correctly-arranged 3D floor plan is already a product people pay for, and it is the foundation everything else sits on.

Phase 0 — Ground truth  2–3 weeks

Before any modelling. Assemble 30–50 real listings with genuine ground truth: photographs, the floor plan, the advertised surface, and — for a subset — actual measurements you have taken yourself, or a Matterport scan you have paid for. Define the metrics now: total area error in %, per-room area error, room adjacency accuracy, layout IoU, and a simple human "would you show this to a buyer" rating. Build the scoring harness before the pipeline.

Note that ZInD, the obvious public dataset, is not licensed for commercial use ✓ VERIFIED, so it can inform your research but should not be the basis of a product's evaluation. Your own set is more work and worth more.

Kill criterion: if you cannot assemble 30 listings with usable ground truth, you do not have a data partner, and without a data partner this is much harder than it needs to be.

Phase 1 — The measurable shell  6–8 weeks

Stages 0, 1, 5, and a simplified 4, 6 and 7. Output: a scaled 3D architectural shell with correct room arrangement, plus an interactive floor plan, delivered as glTF and a Three.js viewer. No photorealism, no splats. Untextured or flat-shaded.

This is deliberately unglamorous and it is the right first product. It is directly comparable to what Archilogic and CubiCasa sell today, it is fully verifiable against ground truth, and — critically — it exercises the stage that is most likely to kill the project (global assembly) before you have invested anything in appearance.

Kill criterion: total area within ±8% and correct room arrangement on 70% of listings that have a floor plan. If you cannot hit that, the rest does not matter.

Phase 2 — Photorealism and the viewer  8–10 weeks

Stages 2, 3 and 8 in full: grouping, pointmap reconstruction, per-room splatting culled against the phase 1 polygons, plus the waypoint viewer with dollhouse and minimap. This is where it starts to feel like a product.

Kill criterion: a blind panel prefers your output to the listing's photo gallery for "getting a feel for the flat" on a majority of listings. If a splat with floaters is worse than just looking at the photos, you have added cost and subtracted value.

Phase 3 — The no-plan branch and hardening  6–8 weeks

Doorway chaining, layout inference, the human-assisted arrangement step, mirror handling, the failure taxonomy and the review console. Most of the reviewer-cost reduction from §8 happens here.

Phase 4 — Generative completion  optional, and I would wait

Only if phases 1–3 are solid, and only within the boundary described in §4.10. The regulatory environment is tightening rather than loosening, and being the company that got it wrong first in this category is an expensive position.

10Law, data and disclosure

This section reports what sources say. It is not legal advice, and several parts could not be verified in this session — see §12. Take proper counsel in France before launch.

Where the training and input images come from

Scraping portal listing images is the obvious shortcut and a poor idea. Zillow's terms explicitly prohibit automated extraction and competitive product development from scraped data ○ REPORTED, and there is live litigation between CoStar and Zillow over listing imagery running into tens of thousands of images ○ REPORTED. The EU text-and-data-mining exception (DSM Directive Articles 3 and 4) is narrower than people assume: Article 3 covers research organisations, Article 4's commercial exception is subject to a machine-readable opt-out, and both require lawful access in the first place ○ REPORTED. A Dutch court reportedly clarified in early 2025 that opt-out signals must be machine-readable to be effective ○ REPORTED.

The clean path is a data partnership with an agency or a portal. It also happens to be the path that gives you ground truth, floor plans, verified surface areas, and a first customer — which is a strong argument for doing it even setting the legal question aside.

The EU AI Act, which became relevant seventeen days ago

Article 50 of the AI Act requires providers of AI systems generating synthetic image, audio or video content to mark that output in a machine-readable format, detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. Exemptions cover assistive editing functions that do not substantially alter the input data. Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026 ✓ VERIFIED — that is already in force as of the date of this report.

Whether a geometric reconstruction is "synthetic content" is arguable; whether a diffusion-generated wall texture is, is much less arguable. The practical implication for your architecture: keep a clean internal boundary between reconstructed and generated surfaces, so that if marking is required you can apply it precisely rather than stamping the whole artefact.

Property marketing and misrepresentation

Two distinct concerns. The first is size: in France the loi Carrez governs the declared surface of a co-owned lot for sale, and misstating it carries real consequences. A 3D model that implies a different size than the legal figure is a problem regardless of any disclaimer, which is another argument for anchoring scale to the published area rather than to a model's estimate.

The second is condition. The research pass surfaced a clear regulatory direction in the US: California now requires conspicuous disclosure on digitally altered listing images and distinguishes permitted brightness correction from prohibited removal of defects; New York's Department of State issued a warning in November 2025 that AI-generated listing images may constitute deceptive advertising; and MLS rules across boards require watermarking and disclosure of virtual staging, with fines ○ REPORTED. I was unable to verify the French and UK equivalents in this session, but the direction is unmistakable and the underlying principle — do not create a misleading impression of the property — is universal.

A design rule that keeps you on the right side of all of this

Never generate anything that changes the perceived size, layout or condition of the property. Generation is acceptable for the appearance of a surface whose existence and position you have actually established; it is not acceptable for anything a buyer would rely on. Build the viewer so that inferred regions read differently — a subtle desaturation or haze — and put a plain-language note on what is photographed and what is inferred. Being visibly honest here is also a marketing asset in a category where buyer distrust of AI imagery is already documented.

GDPR

Interior photographs routinely contain personal data: family photographs on walls, documents on desks, faces reflected in mirrors, sometimes people. If you are storing and reprocessing agency image sets you are a processor at minimum. Detection-and-blurring of personal items is worth building early — it is also a feature agencies will want independently.

12Caveats on this report

Worth knowing how this was made, so you can weigh it properly.

  • Research was gathered by eleven parallel search agents. Nine returned substantive results; two hit the session's web-search limit and returned nothing — the topics they covered were vision-language model spatial reasoning and European legal specifics. I filled part of both gaps directly, but §10's French and UK content is thinner than it should be, and you should treat it as a prompt for proper research rather than a summary of one.
  • The search budget was exhausted before verification, so I confirmed load-bearing claims by fetching primary sources directly. Everything marked ✓ VERIFIED I read myself. Everything marked ○ REPORTED I did not.
  • Benchmark numbers deserve particular scepticism. Search agents are prone to reproducing figures with more precision than the sources support, and several of the 2026 arXiv identifiers that came back were not confirmed to exist. Before you cite any specific accuracy figure from §4, open the paper.
  • Two claims in §6 come from my own knowledge rather than this session's research and are flagged as needing verification: the licence status of the original Inria 3DGS implementation versus gsplat, and the Magic Leap licence on SuperPoint weights. Both are the kind of trap that is expensive to discover late, so check them early.
  • Cost figures in §8 are my estimates from stated inference times, not measurements. The human-review observation is a modelling argument, not an empirical finding — but it is the one I would most encourage you to test early, because it changes where you spend effort.

If it is useful, the natural next step is a short scoping pass on phase 0: which agencies or portals you could realistically partner with in France, and what thirty listings with ground truth would actually take to assemble. That decision shapes everything downstream more than any technical choice in this document.
